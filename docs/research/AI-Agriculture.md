## 1. Inside Holland's Smart Tomato Farms: How AI Is Revolutionizing Agriculture

AI grows tomatoes inside Holland's smart greenhouses. In a country smaller than many US states, the Netherlands has quietly become one of the world's biggest tomato exporters. But it's not just farmers making this possible. It's artificial intelligence. Agriculture here no longer depends on seasons or sunlight. It runs on algorithms, sensors, and precision data. Welcome to the smart farms of Holland, where AI literally grows your food.

Every tomato here is part of a living network of data. These futuristic greenhouses, often called glass cities, are filled with sensors that track humidity, temperature, light intensity, and carbon dioxide in real time. Thousands of readings flow into AI systems that instantly adjust the environment. If humidity rises, vents open automatically. If sunlight fades, LEDs adjust their brightness and color. Every variable is tuned to create the perfect growing climate. This is data-driven farming. Plants don't rely on guesswork anymore. Their growth is calculated, optimized, and predicted.

AI models process millions of data points to learn what each plant needs at every stage of its life. They even analyze subtle color changes in the leaves to detect stress or nutrient deficiency before the human eye can notice. In some of these Dutch farms, the farmer is not a person. It's a neural network. Machine learning algorithms study years of growth data, comparing patterns between millions of tomato plants to forecast the perfect harvest time. These systems can predict how many kilograms of tomatoes will be ready for picking days or even weeks in advance.

And when harvest time comes, robots take the lead. Inside the glowing glass structures, robotic arms move gently through the rows, trimming, pollinating, and picking ripe tomatoes with surgical precision. Overhead, AI-guided drones scan the crops from above, capturing hyperspectral images that reveal plant health, disease signs, and growth rates. The drones don't just see — they understand.

The result of this technological symphony is astonishing: up to 90% less water consumption, near-zero pesticide use, and a consistent high-quality yield all year round. Each tomato is grown in a perfectly controlled microclimate, fine-tuned by algorithms that learn and improve every single day.

But what makes these farms even more powerful is their connection to the cloud. Every greenhouse is part of a digital ecosystem, uploading terabytes of data to centralized platforms. There, AI analyzes performance across hundreds of farms, spotting patterns humans could never detect. It predicts how global climate changes might affect yield and helps farmers plan months ahead. This is predictive agriculture — farming guided not by weather, but by data.

Efficiency is everything. Smart irrigation systems deliver exactly the amount of water each plant needs, drop by drop. If a tomato plant absorbs less moisture on a cloudy day, the system adjusts instantly. Nothing is wasted. Solar-powered heating and ventilation systems recycle energy, while advanced CO₂ sensors ensure plants grow faster without harming the environment. Sustainability isn't just a goal — it's built into the system's DNA.

Yet, for all its promise, this new form of farming faces real challenges. Building and maintaining a smart greenhouse can cost millions. Integrating robotics, AI, and data systems requires constant technical expertise. And then there's the question of cybersecurity. When your farm depends on the internet, a single breach could destroy an entire harvest.

Still, the Netherlands treats these challenges as opportunities. Universities, startups, and tech firms collaborate to create resilient, modular greenhouse systems that can adapt to any region or climate. Dutch companies are now exporting this technology to more than 150 countries, turning the nation into a global model for sustainable agriculture.

In many ways, the Netherlands has become the Silicon Valley of food. It's not just exporting tomatoes — it's exporting the future of farming itself. From Japan's vertical farms to the UAE's desert greenhouses, Dutch innovation is reshaping how the world feeds itself.

And this is only the beginning. The next generation of farms won't need human farmers inside. AI systems will monitor, decide, and harvest autonomously while humans act as architects of digital ecosystems that grow life. The farmer of the future might not wear boots, but code. In this transformation, intelligence becomes the new soil and algorithms become the new sunlight.

From the heart of Europe, the Netherlands is proving that food doesn't have to come from nature alone. It can come from knowledge, precision, and data. Artificial intelligence is no longer just analyzing the world — it's helping to grow it.

## 3. The Smart Farm Revolution: AI Meets Agriculture | DW News

> Agricultural machinery that destroys weeds using laser beams — new AI software makes it possible.

In northern Germany, a new machine rolls across an onion field, burning away weeds with laser beams. Controlled by AI, this groundbreaking technology targets only unwanted plants. All that's left is a fine layer of ash.

It heats the soil a lot, and operators need to be careful not to get burned by the laser beam. The machine delivers around 24 kW of optical power, with each laser at 2 kW — enough to cause deep tissue burns within milliseconds if you put your hand beneath it.

Stefan Hinrich invented the machine. Growing up on a farm, he knew the problems weeds cause and wanted a solution. A generator at the front of the tractor powers the laser beams, while satellite navigation guides it across the field. The core innovation is the AI software Hinrich developed as part of his doctoral thesis. The camera system can distinguish between crops and weeds, and control the laser beam with pinpoint accuracy. The software recognizes every stage of plant growth and stores millions of bits of information which it can retrieve again and again. The cameras are mounted underneath the housing and the software guides the laser beams to exactly where they're needed.

> "We work outdoors where environmental conditions constantly change. Some weeds are larger, some smaller, with different leaf shapes. Adjusting all these parameters is very complex. With AI, many of them can be learned and controlled much more easily. That's why AI is a small revolution in this field."

Only a handful of companies worldwide have mastered and commercialized this technology. Its development was time-consuming and costly, but it's already paying off — especially for carrots and onions, and in regions where labor is expensive and pesticide use is being reduced.

Stefan Hinrich's partner, Tomas Tangel, works in the new production hall at their company headquarters in Bavaria. The two friends and colleagues founded the company four years ago and have since developed a new type of mechanical hoeing machine. It destroys weeds between crop rows with pinpoint accuracy, also using their own AI software. The two developers started out in the workshop on their parents' farm, writing software and welding their first machines.

> "We like building things, developing something new for farmers. You get to see more than you do as a farmer. It's more fun. You meet others, learn about their problems, and make friends who want to help even more."

Many farmers struggle with a shortage of workers, especially for jobs like sorting potatoes — tough, dirty work that few people want to do. That's where AI software comes in. It detects rocks, clumps of soil, and rotten potatoes, and removes them faster than any person could. The machine handles 40 tons per hour — a full truckload — saving the labor of four workers.

The AI software can be used in a wide range of agricultural machines. The goal now is to move toward large-scale manufacturing so more farms can benefit from this technology.

> "No sponsors, no backing. We built all of this on our own. We're not tied to any company or group. It's just the two of us."

AI software is set to transform agricultural technology in the years ahead. For now, the system is still in its infancy.

## 4. The Future of Farming | DW Shift

Is this what rural life will look like tomorrow? Robots replacing workers, and farmers managing their operations through apps and the internet. In some barns, robots are already being used for feeding, while AI cameras monitor the animals' health. And satellite-guided tractors are driving autonomously across the fields.

### Online farming education — Bangladesh

Online training for farmers via social media: this is how a young woman in Bangladesh has successfully built her business. She also markets the agricultural products herself, grown according to her specifications.

> "I am Umma Kulsum Popi, an agri-entrepreneur, and I create agriculture-based videos to build awareness about safe farming."

Popi's goal is to teach safe farming — how to grow crops and fruits without the harmful pesticides that can put health at risk.

> "If mangos are bagged like this using fruit bags, there is no need to spray pesticides on them for a long time. Also, the mangos remain fresh and safe to eat."

> "I don't feel safe eating fruit that I buy outside because I am unsure what's in it. When excessive pesticides are used in the food we consume during cultivation, some chemicals remain and cause diseases like kidney damage. Many people get cancer now, and one of the reasons behind that is the use of pesticides during cultivation."

But it's not just about health — it's also about protecting the environment. In a densely populated, flood-prone country like Bangladesh, that matters a lot. So, in 2022, Popi started making videos showing safer, smarter ways to farm. A popular Bangladeshi TV show about agriculture helped her figure out how to tell stories that farmers would actually watch.

> "As our team was small when I started making video content, I had to film stories using the phone I had in my hand during my visit to a farm or chats with farmers. I thought if I filmed and shared the information I learned from them online, many people would benefit from it."

Today, Popi has more than 4.5 million followers across Facebook, YouTube, TikTok, and Instagram. She posts 5–7 short clips a week. The popular influencer now has a team to keep the content flowing.

Popi says she's not promoting brands in her videos — she's promoting her own business. The agri-entrepreneur has built a network of farmers who now sell their produce to her company at a fair price. Her goal is to make safe farming both practical and profitable. By paying farmers fairly, she helps remove the pressure to use harmful pesticides.

> "We tried to solve their existing problems, hoping that then they would try to follow our advice on using safe farming methods."

With income from her fruit company and ad revenue from her videos, Popi has built a stable business. She now employs several people. She is only 26 — and already planning her next move: a training center to take her agri-lessons from online to in person.

In Bangladesh, around 45% of all workers are still employed in agriculture, and many of them do hard physical labor.

### Dairy farm automation — Europe

In the European Union, agriculture is highly automated and less than 5% of the workforce is employed in the sector. In many regions, it has become difficult to find people willing to do the work.

Automated systems are now also used in dairy farms, where animals no longer have names, only numbers. No humans work in one such barn — 170 cattle are cared for by robots around the clock. They are fed four times a day, with AI controlling the exact amount. These feeding systems cost around €100,000 but pay off quickly. With labor shortages in Europe, robots are cheaper in the long run.

Outside, feed is prepared automatically and delivered fresh into the barn. This allows the farmer to run the entire operation alone. His most important tool is his smartphone with specialized AI software. Each animal wears a collar with a sensor connected to the app, constantly providing data on every cow.

The milking robot makes the biggest difference. Using sensors, it guides the milking equipment to the udder. The cows quickly adapt and go to the station on their own several times a day.

> "Without technology, this farm wouldn't exist anymore because the workload would be unmanageable. You'd need at least one more person. At some point, you start questioning whether it's worth it — you want to have a life too. And as long as the technology is enjoyable and works, it's a great solution."

The robot technology comes from an Austrian company. Demand for agricultural automation is rising worldwide, with machines used in Brazil, the Middle East, and Australia. From one global control center, technicians can access any device worldwide, as long as the internet connection is stable, and check exactly what each system is doing — or not doing.

In more and more barns, robots are taking over physically demanding routine tasks. Farming is becoming highly specialized and globally connected. While farmers work the field, they can still monitor their animals via an online platform — receiving information about their livestock on their phone around the clock. This allows farming to function with fewer and fewer people.

### Autonomous tractor — India

Farming methods that are sustainable and efficient are not only an important topic in Europe. The mechanization of the agricultural sector is also advancing in Asia. In the northern Indian state of Haryana, the rapid transformation of agriculture is on display. After generations of hard, manual labor, this farm now uses an autonomous tractor — self-learning, highly precise, and cutting the farmer's workload in half.

## 5. Artificial Intelligence Comes to Farming in India | BBC News

In India, 50% of the workforce are employed in agriculture, and a growing population of now 1.4 billion are dependent on the crops produced. But traditional methods are now making way for new technology that promises to make farms more efficient, productive, and profitable, thanks to artificial intelligence.

For generations, Indian farms have been sown and tilled only with traditional know-how. But some, like Nandkishore Patel, are trying out something different — with sensor devices on his vineyard that check weather and soil health, and use artificial intelligence to figure out when to water the crops, add fertilizer, and tackle pests. He then receives a precise advisory on a mobile app.

> "This vineyard has no groundwater sources and we are growing these vines with the water that we purchase from outside tankers. With the help of this AI data, we are now able to irrigate them only at the crucial stage, and that's helping us to save around 50% of the water that we actually used to give before."

Built in India's Silicon Valley — Bangalore — by startup Fil AgriTech, the service has led to a near 25% boost in productivity on crops like grapes and guavas.

Informed decision-making is only one part of the solution to improve productivity — but weeding out inefficiencies in existing age-old agricultural practices is also crucial. AI-powered robots offer a solution. One robot is equipped with precision cameras that scan the ground in real time, programmed to avoid false spraying.

> "The way spraying is done in India is on an acre level, and our mission is to bring that down to a plant-level decision. Just by spraying only on the plant, we are seeing a 56% savings."

Improved rural digital connectivity and government support for AgriTech startups has pushed farm innovations. But even now, just 2% of cultivators use technology in farming.

> "We really need to enable the digital public infrastructure. And the second is going to be public-private partnerships. India will always be resource-constrained — we are perhaps lowest in yield levels, pretty much constrained on finance and insurance services for the farmers. And that's where the gap needs to be filled up."

AI data-driven agriculture promises profitability, but will need considerable time and investment to reach the majority of India's farmers.

## 6. On the Ground with Farming's Robot Revolution | Financial Times

> The FT gets hands-on with smaller bots that promise to protect soil quality, reduce the use of pesticides, and boost agricultural productivity. They could eventually change the industry and the landscape beyond recognition.

We're on the verge of a farming robot revolution. Investors poured $700 million into agricultural technology in 2017 alone. But are farm bots ready to deliver? The FT visited Leckford Estate in the south of England to see how one start-up is working to change the face of farming.

### Monitoring robot

> "So this is Tom, our monitoring robot. At the moment we're in a trials phase. Tom is looking at the wheat crop that we have here and gathering data on it — analysing where the weeds are, looking at the health status of that plant, looking at any disease issues, nutrient issues. He's just taking high-quality digital images top-down at the moment. And that goes into Wilma, who's our AI."

Wilma's artificial intelligence matches the images to precise GPS data, generating a map that's accurate to within two centimetres. The farmer can then zoom right in on specific plants, potentially isolating problems before they can spread.

For Andrew Hoad, the head of Leckford Estate, it's a game changer.

> "Leckford Estate is a 4,000-acre estate — that's about 1,800 hectares. With such a large area, we can't realistically cover every part of the estate. So having a bit of machinery that is in a field, able to tell us in real time what is going on, enables us to be much more responsive and be more precise about what we are doing."

The technology still has a way to go. The Tom prototype struggles to cover some of the farm's more rugged terrain, and in its current form, it can't even go out in the rain.

### Impact on the environment

> "Like any farm, we are facing into higher energy costs. Conventional farming also relies heavily on inputs. And the ability to have very targeted interventions — because you've got that visibility of that single plant — will mean that there's potentially the opportunity, from the research that the Small Robot Company have done, to take out about 60% of the input cost. Now, that's not just a cost thing. That's a really good thing for the environment."

That kind of payoff will only be possible if farmers replace their tractors and combines with a new breed of technology — smart bots, light and nimble enough to seed, weed, and feed individual plants without damaging others in the same field. For the Small Robot Company, this is the next phase.

### Conclusion

> "Looking at Harry here, you will notice that Harry has the capacity not just to carry a boom for planting, but also another boom underneath for electric weeding, or another boom underneath for some clever way of being able to kill pests which doesn't need chemicals. We are a starting point to allow all sorts of clever technologies to be applied for arable farmers."

Moving past this starting point will take innovation and funding — both of which may be harder to come by, given the low unit value of staple crops like wheat and soy that this bot is designed for.

> "Where we're going to really drive the large increases in food production at a sustainable level is going to happen in arable. And there, unfortunately, there is much less interest — because from a start-up perspective, the value is less — which means we've got a much longer journey to be able to get there."

Small Robot Company may still be years from delivering on the full potential of farm bots. But if their bots can replace the heavy machinery of today's large-scale farming, they could change the industry and the landscape beyond recognition.

## 7. Revolutionising Food Production with the Small Robot Company | IET

> In March 2018, a British agri-tech start-up became the latest winner of the prestigious Horizontal Innovation Award from the IET and the High Value Manufacturing Catapult (HVMC), enabling the company to develop 'Harry', its new drilling and planting robot. Based in Shropshire, the Small Robot Company harnesses the power and precision of robotics and AI to improve food production through its future fleet of farming robots: Tom, Dick, and Harry.

### Introduction

A small robot company — a UK-based AgriTech start-up — is looking to replace big tractors with small robots. They believe that where tractors are neither efficient nor environmentally friendly, their small robots will seed and care for each individual plant in a crop.

### Problems with modern farming

> "There are some big problems with the way that we farm and produce food at the moment. We're producing things in quite an inaccurate way — farming by averages. When we're applying chemicals to a field, a big chunk of that fertilizer is never being taken up by the plant. It's either evaporating into the atmosphere or going into the soil and being washed away, never really coming into contact with the roots of the plant."

> "Currently, 95% of energy is used in ploughing, and ploughing is only necessary because of heavy machinery crushing soil. The current machinery — the tools that we use to farm — are no longer enabling an increase in productivity. These machines are extremely heavy, which has a negative impact on the soil — they're compacting the soil, which restricts root growth. There's an $800 billion a year damage bill from farming."

### The Small Robot Company approach

> "We, as a small robot company, are trying to maximize the way that we use technology to produce food. We recognized that there was a big gap between what was technically possible and what was actually happening on farm, and we started to think there's a better way of doing things — that the technology could enable a system that, rather than considering the field as a unit, considered the individual plant as a unit, and then treated each plant as an individual item and applied fertilizer much more accurately."

> "We spent a long time going around and interviewing farmers. We've interviewed over a hundred farmers now. They were very into the idea of technology, but they're really scared about spending hundreds of thousands of pounds on a new piece of kit that they didn't understand. So we wanted to explore a way to really lower that barrier to entry and make sure that farmers can get involved and take advantage of it without those big hurdles of cost and the fear of things going wrong."

In March 2018, the Small Robot Company was announced as winner of the prestigious Horizontal Innovation Award, to develop Harry — the company's new drilling and planting robot. The IET has teamed up with the MTC to deliver its Horizontal Innovation Programme.

### Horizontal innovation explained

Horizontal innovation is the process of having a problem in your field and looking outside your sector to other areas for a solution. The Small Robot Company's application was the perfect example: they took the concept of robotics from service robotics and industrial manufacturing and applied it to agriculture — packaging existing technologies into a new system to help tackle food productivity, waste, and pollution.

### The robot fleet: Tom, Dick, and Harry

> "We developed a system of three small robots. Tom is the monitoring robot — Tom goes into a field, analysing each individual plant, understanding whether there are nutrient issues, disease issues in that field, and where exactly they are. Dick is a micro-spraying and non-chemical weeding robot — taking the data gathered by Tom and going out into the field and applying chemicals in minute doses, just to the plants that need it. And then the final robot is Harry — a digital planter, placing an individual seed in the ground and varying the seed spacing and seed depth based on the current understanding of best practice."

> "The idea behind Harry is that he doesn't need to plant into a ploughed field — he can just punch-plant directly into the soil, directly into the stubble of the previous year's crop. There are huge advantages of this for the environment. If you leave the soil alone and you don't stomp up and down on it, it will automatically look after itself — the worms will come back and you'll get a much better growing medium for the plants. The other thing about doing punch planting is that every time you plant a seed you know exactly where it is, which means we can provide a per-plant map of the entire crop."

### The Rachel prototype

The Small Robot Company already has one robot working in the fields, named Rachel — a prototype Tom robot.

> "Rachel is a really good testbed for us to try out some of the things that Tom needs to be able to do. She has a very accurate GNSS system that can give us a two-centimetre round location fix. Also on the robot we have a mobile phone on the back that enables us to have a Wi-Fi hotspot if we've got 3G or 4G connectivity in the field. Inside we have basically a battery and a Raspberry Pi, and also some Arduino-based hardware which we use for interacting with the wheel encoders and other electronics on board. We make extensive use of the ROS robot operating system framework, which really means that we don't have to redo all the common things that people who make robots have to do — we can make use of those common components and then build our own smarts on top."

The IET award was a fantastic opportunity, and what really made it work was the first time the team went to the Manufacturing Technology Centre in Coventry — the MTC's view was to apply a completely ground-up functional design process in an area where very few are thinking about it at that level.

> "For us it's been vital to what we're doing. The opportunity to essentially create this new farming revolution allows rural economies to become more diversified, as jobs move towards taking part in the actual agricultural business, which is an intrinsic part of the local community."

## 8. FarmBeats Tracks Soil and Moisture Data 24/7 | Microsoft Research

> FarmBeats is an agriculture research project developed by Microsoft. It uses solar-powered, white-space-based internet connectivity to record soil temperature and moisture levels and track them with cloud-based computing models. FarmBeats enables data-driven farming in remote areas through the use of inexpensive monitoring equipment, including cameras, to help increase the food yield of farms.

One of the world's problems is yield. How do you double yield by 2050 to feed a growing world population? FarmBeats is a project recently started with the goal of enabling data-driven farming.

> "The first goal is to give a real-time view of the farm — the real-time pulse of the farm — to the farmer, saying: here is what your farm is doing right now. But how do you even connect these farms to the internet? It's so far remote. The cell carriers have very little incentive to provide connectivity on the farms."

The system deployed at Dancing Crow Farm in Washington measures soil temperature and moisture, coming from sensors connected to a white-spaces device. A farmer buys a white-space router, plugs it in, and the entire farm is connected. Multiple devices and sensors can now be deployed cheaply, getting a real-time view of all parts of the farm — not only soil and moisture data, but through the high-bandwidth connection, cameras can provide real-time views from the farm.

> "Once you have all this data, you could then analyze it to predict what the future would look like. This last year has been mainly a data-gathering, information-collecting year for the Microsoft Research team. I'm really looking forward to seeing what research they've gathered, putting that together with what I think is going to happen next year, and making predictions for what I should plant, when, and where."

## 9. How Data-Driven Farming Could Transform Agriculture | TEDx | Ranveer Chandra

> Microsoft researcher Dr. Ranveer Chandra discusses his team's mission to reinvent rural agriculture. Using cost-effective IoT applications, Chandra outlines how providing wide-ranging crop data to farmers can reduce time, money, and resources while simultaneously increasing crop yields — a potential solution to future worldwide food shortages.

### The food problem

The world's food production needs to increase by 70% by 2050 to feed a growing population — and this is just to feed, not nourish. The problem is severe because the amount of arable land is limited and water levels are receding. It's like the Green Revolution problem all over again: how do we get to this significant increase in food production?

### Data-driven agriculture

The most promising approach right now seems to be data-driven farming — the ability to map every farm in the world and overlay it with lots and lots of data. For example: what is the soil moisture level six inches below the soil throughout the farm? What is the soil nutrient level throughout the farm?

If you could build maps like this, it could enable precision agriculture — the ability to do site-specific applications. Right now, farmers apply water uniformly throughout the farm. They apply pesticide uniformly. With precision agriculture, you could apply it only where it is needed. Precision agriculture as a technique has been shown to improve yield and reduce cost, because farmers use less water and less pesticide. It's also better for the environment — you're not putting in more nitrogen or wasting water than needed.

The other technique such maps could enable is phenotyping. Just as you could do genotyping, you could do phenotyping as well — understanding why the same seed variety grew differently in different parts of the farm, and creating new genotypes as a result.

Precision agriculture as a technique was first proposed back in the 1980s. It's been 30 years since then, and the technology hasn't taken off. The biggest reason is cost. At a recent expo, the cheapest sensors available were five sensors for $8,000 — a recurring cost that is expecting too much from farmers, especially when the return on investment is unclear. That is the goal of the FarmBeats project: to bring down the cost of data-driven agriculture solutions by two orders of magnitude — from $8,000 to $80.

### Internet connectivity

The first challenge is internet connectivity. The farms don't have connectivity, so existing solutions end up using satellite or custom cellular solutions to connect devices to the internet. To bring down the cost of connectivity, FarmBeats uses TV white spaces.

TV white spaces use empty over-the-air TV channels. When you browse over-the-air TV, certain channels show nothing but white noise — TV white spaces fit a Wi-Fi signal into those empty channels without interfering with adjacent channels. Compared to Wi-Fi at the same power level, UHF TV frequencies carry signals four times farther; in VHF, twelve times farther. Put trees, crops, and canopies in the way, and signals keep going through. In experiments, sensors buried a meter under soil still maintained signal.

This technology was made legal in the US in 2010 and has since been deployed to connect rural hospitals, schools, and libraries to the internet. In agriculture, the key insight is that TV towers are where people are. Farms are away from cities — so if you go to a farm and turn on an over-the-air TV, you'll find very few channels. Most are just white noise. The more empty channels, the more capacity. At a farm, you have hundreds of megabits per second of unused spectrum — enough to connect not just sensors, but cameras, drones, and tractors.

If you talk to any agricultural scientist, the number one problem they'll mention is data — how do you get data from the middle of the farm? With TV white spaces, you can solve that problem. Just like Wi-Fi connects your house, TV white spaces can connect your entire farm.

### Building accurate soil maps with few sensors

The second challenge is coverage. To build an accurate soil moisture map, you'd need a sensor every 10 meters — expensive to deploy, expensive to manage, and disruptive to the farmer's daily work. The key question is: can you build such a map using very few sensors?

FarmBeats solves this using UAVs — drones that can fly large areas very quickly with a camera at the bottom to take images of the entire farm. The key technology, built using artificial intelligence and machine learning, uses aerial images to interpolate data from a few sensors and predict values across the entire farm.

The key insight: if two parts of the farm look similar in RGB, hyperspectral, or multispectral imagery, they are likely to have similar soil values. This approach has been shown to work well for soil moisture, soil temperature, and pH.

For parts of the world where drones are too expensive or require Ministry of Defense permission to fly, FarmBeats uses a low-tech alternative: tethered helium balloons. These fly 200–250 feet and can take continuous aerial imagery for up to four to seven days. A custom mount holds a smartphone and battery pack, continuously capturing images. In places like India and Africa, someone could just walk around with the balloon, or put it on a bike or tractor, and computer vision algorithms stitch the images together into large aerial maps of the entire farm.

The pipeline: aerial image from drone or balloon → full farm photograph → raw sensor data → machine learning model → prediction of soil values across the entire farm. This has been demonstrated for pH, moisture, and temperature, and is being extended to other variables.

Combining TV white spaces for connectivity with aerial imagery for coverage, FarmBeats can bring down the cost of each sensor and dramatically reduce the number of sensors needed to build accurate maps.

### Connectivity from the farm to the cloud

The third challenge is the last-mile connection from the farmer's house to the cloud. Many farmers pay for broadband but only get 1–3 Mbps. Flying a drone for 15 minutes generates over a gigabyte of data — you can't send that over a 1–3 Mbps connection in time. The connection is also prone to outages, especially in winter.

FarmBeats solves this by running everything in a box on the farmer's own PC. The box takes data from sensors and drones via the TV white spaces, regenerates the aerial images, runs the machine learning, generates heat maps — all in the farmer's house itself. It then feeds the results into agricultural services. Detailed drone imagery stays local; only a compressed version is sent to the cloud. The system can also run offline.

### Results from real farms

Deployed at farms in Washington state and upstate New York, FarmBeats provides insights within 30 minutes of flying the drone. In one case, a kilometer-stretch drone flight revealed: grass growing back well from left to right, a water puddle needing to be fixed before the next planting season, and the locations of individual cattle — including a stray cow that needed to be herded in.

Soil moisture maps can flag that the top-left corner of a farm is still moist even without a sensor over there. After a farmer applied lime, pH maps flagged dark patches that were still acidic. Accuracy tests across three farms and five-acre plots showed that using just 10 sensors to predict 990 additional data points produced measurements actionable by the farmer — close enough to actual sensor readings to matter.

Cameras in barns streaming data via TV white spaces were able to flag whether cows are moving around well or whether a cow is sick.

FarmBeats aims to take these technologies to smallholder farmers everywhere in the world — bringing the same data-driven agriculture capabilities to the farms of India, Africa, and beyond that large commercial farms enjoy today.
